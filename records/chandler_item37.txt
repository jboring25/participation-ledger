MICAH MIRANDA, Economic Development Director, provided an overview of the development 
agreement outlining the performance obligations for the proposed AI data center and 
accompanying tech park. He explained that the agreement requires the data center to use 
mechanical or closed -loop cooling systems only, with water use capped at the Tier 1 allocation 
under the sustainable water use agreement. No water -based cooling may be used after the 
certificate of occupancy unless it is in a closed-loop system. Existing water connections to cooling 
towers must be removed and capped during decommissioning, and thereafter , the site may be 
served only by 1-inch water meters. Mr. Miranda noted that demolition of existing buildings must 
begin within 90 days after approval or within five days of power being disconnected. Demolition 
must be completed within 18 months. Construction of Tech Park Buildings E and F must begin by 
January 2031, or the developer will incur penalties of $200,000 per year; Buildings C and D must 
begin by January 2032 or face $225,000 per year in penalties. All electric lines serving the site must 
Page 15 of 39 
 
 
be placed underground. To safeguard city revenues, the agreement ensures that property tax and 
TPT revenues cannot decrease due to state or federal incentives; future payments must remain 
at the highest level and adjust for inflation. Employment requirements include a minimum of two 
full-time employees per 1,000 square feet, with a median wage of at least $75,000. Jobs must be 
maintained for five years after each building receives its certificate of occupancy. Annual reporting 
is required, and the developer  will be assessed $2,000 per missing job per year if employment 
falls below the minimum.  The facility must be used exclusively for AI and advanced computing 
data center operations; traditional enterprise data centers, colocation services, and cloud storage 
facilities are prohibited. Mr. Miranda concluded by noting that the development agreement has a 
10-year term with an automatic 10-year renewal. 
 
MAYOR HARTKE asked for an explanation of the power arrangements with SRP. 
 
MR. MIRANDA reported that the applicant must enter a formal contract with SRP, expected to be 
finalized in late fall or early December. That agreement will define utility needs and the phased 
delivery of power to the site, anticipated around 2031–2032. 
 
MAYOR HARTKE requested clarification regarding incorrect media reports on water.  
 
SIMONE KJOLSRUD, Water Resources Manager, explained that the project uses closed- loop 
cooling, which requires very little water. Confusion in reporting may have stemmed from the 
standard application language used for unidentified future tenants in shell buildings. She clarified 
that the data center would be limited to about 48,000 gallons per day, and the remaining five tech-
park buildings would be limited to roughly 30,000 gallons per day (about 5,000–6,000 per building), 
unless they later requested more water under the city’s sustainable allocation policy. 
 
MAYOR HARTKE noted the site is restricted to a one -inch water line, which physically caps the 
maximum deliverable water. 
 
MS. KJOLSRUD explained that a one-inch water meter can only deliver roughly 50,000 gallons per 
day, depending on the specific meter, and the site cannot physically receive more than that. If the 
developer ever sought additional water capacity, they would need to return to Council, request a 
larger meter, and undergo the city’s sustainable allocation process. 
 
MAYOR HARTKE clarified that such a request would be a Council decision, not a staff-level decision. 
 
MS. KJOLSRUD confirmed that it would indeed require Council approval. 
 
COUNCILMEMBER HARRIS asked what the total water allocation for the site would be under 
standard business use and whether a different type of business would require a similar, greater, 
or lesser amount.  
 
Page 16 of 39 
 
 
MS. KJOLSRUD responded that the allocation reflects the c ity’s standard water allowance for any 
commercial or industrial business. The maximum allocation is 48,000 gallons per day, which is 
likely more than a data center would require due to its closed-loop cooling system. This maximum 
is based on the building’s square footage and represents a typical water use level for a building of 
that size in Chandler. 
 
COUNCILMEMBER HARRIS asked whether Chandler residents subsidize business water use. 
 
MS. KJOLSRUD explained that the c ity conducts a cost -of-service study every two years for each 
category of water users. Water rates are set based on the impact each user category has on the 
system, and each category pays accordingly. 
 
COUNCILMEMBER HARRIS asked whether any new business connected to Chandler’s 
infrastructure would affect residents’ water rates in the future. 
 
MS. KJOLSRUD explained that all water users fund the maintenance of existing infrastructure . 
Costs for aging pipes are included in the city’s cost of service. Any new or special infrastructure 
required by a development would typically be addressed through a development agreement. 
 
COUNCILMEMBER HARRIS asked about the site’s current water usage.  
 
MS. KJOLSRUD stated that the site currently uses approximately one million gallons per month 
due to existing evaporative cooling systems for a vacant building. Under the proposed 
redevelopment, those systems would be removed, and new mechanically based, closed- loop 
cooling systems that use little to no water would be installed.  
 
COUNCILMEMBER ORLANDO stated that he was reviewing Development Agreement UDM- 121 
and sought clarification on the water use figures, noting an average annual daily use of 48,631 
gallons and a maximum use of 97,262 gallons, and asked what those numbers represented.  
 
MS. KJOLSRUD explained that the figures came from the applicant’s submission estimating water 
use for the data center. She stated that both numbers represent maximums under the c ity’s Tier 
One sustainable water allocation. The city requires two figures: average daily water use calculated 
across the entire year, and maximum daily use representing peak demand. 
 
COUNCILMEMBER ORLANDO confirmed that the figures were gallons per day and asked whether 
the average was based on 365 days. 
 
MS. KJOLSRUD confirmed and explained that the 48,000 -gallon figure reflects total annual use 
divided by 365, while the 97,000-gallon figure reflects a possible single-day peak, with lower usage 
balancing the annual average.

COUNCILMEMBER ORLANDO noted that multiplying 48,000 gallons by 365 would exceed 12

daily allotment, not the anticipated actual usage, and establishes a  cap that the applicant cannot 
exceed. 
 
COUNCILMEMBER POSTON asked whether staff had estimated the economic impact of the

MR. MIRANDA responded that an economic impact analysis had been completed. He stated that

additional $10.4 million attributable to indirect and induced impacts.  
 
COUNCILMEMBER POSTON asked for clarification on the difference between direct and indirect 
impacts.  
 
MR. MIRANDA explained that direct impacts result from the project’s business operations, while 
indirect impacts reflect secondary economic activity generated by employees and related 
spending. He noted that the difference between the figures is relatively small because data 
centers employ few people and generate limited ancillary spending.

COUNCILMEMBER POSTON raised concerns about the project’s water use, citing claims that AI 
data centers require significantly more water due to higher energy demand, and asked city staff 
to clarify whether this was accurate.  
 
MR. BACH stated that the claim was inaccurate and added that the development agreement would 
prohibit such water use even if it were true. 
 
COUNCILMEMBER POSTON asked city staff to address the issue.  
 
MS. KJOLSRUD explained that there is some truth to the concern, depending on where water use 
occurs. She stated that the claim is not accurate with respect to Chandler’s water resources. She  
noted that the Salt River Project (SRP) uses water to generate power. She explained that SRP’s 
power generation is primarily hydropower, which is considered very sustainable. SRP releases 
water from dams to spin turbines and generate electricity  and uses a system of pumping water 
during periods of low energy demand and releasing it during periods of higher energy demand . 
As a result, while water is used to generate additional power, it does not come from Chandler’s 
water supplies.
